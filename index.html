01 — The Problem

Why this matters.

According to Singapore's National Registry of Diseases, the incidence rate of unintentional falls among adults over 60 is 277.7 per 100,000 — and many of these happen at home, often alone. When no one is nearby, even a minor fall can become life-threatening.

Existing solutions fall short.Emergency buttons need to be physically reached. Smartphones assume the user is mobile and tech-literate.

This project takes a different approach: a passive, always-on device designed to sit near a bathroom — statistically one of the highest-risk areas for elderly falls. No button to press. Traditional AI systems rely heavily on cloud processing and internet connectivity. WHY TinyML ? TinyML enables machine learning models to run directly on low-power embedded devices, allowing faster response times, enhanced privacy, and offline operation.

The phrase "Help me now" was chosen deliberately as it only has three syllables, easy to distinguish from background speech, and naturally translatable across Singapore's three main languages: English, Mandarin, and Malay.

02 — Prototype

See it work.

The prototype uses three LEDs as visual indicators — each colour mapped to a different language. When the model detects the distress phrase with sufficient confidence, the corresponding LED lights up within milliseconds.

Tested across 7 different voices — the model successfully detected the correct phrase on the first attempt in 5 out of 7 cases. The three-language design was deliberate: Singapore's elderly community spans English, Mandarin, and Malay speakers. A device that only understands one language would exclude the majority of those it's meant to protect.

03 — Inside the Build

Hardware & software.

TinyML is the practice of running machine learning models on microcontrollers. Devices with kilobytes of RAM, not gigabytes. Design specically to run on low-powered energy consumption.

Feature Prototype

Distress Recognition System

A low-powered device using the Arduino Nano BLE 33 that recognises a distress phrase and activates a colour-coded LED alert system in real time. Unlike conventional emergency systems requiring manual activation, this project utilizes embedded TinyML speech recognition to autonomously detect verbal distress signals in real time while maintaining low power consumption and offline functionality

  • ◈ Noise-robust detection - model trained on apartment soundscapes including TV, traffic, and background conversation
  • ◈ Self-collected multilingual dataset - audio samples recorded and labelled from scratch
  • ◈ MFCC feature extraction via Edge Impulse, deployed as a C++ inference library
  • ◈ Operates fully on-device without reliance on cloud computation

04 — The Process

How it was built.

Initial research focused on TinyML, embedded speech recognition, and low-power edge AI systems to identify an effective approach for real-time offline distress detection. The development process involved continuous experimentation, testing, and optimisation to balance detection accuracy, inference speed, memory usage, and power efficiency. Throughout the project, several hardware and software limitations were encountered, requiring iterative troubleshooting and refinement before achieving a stable and reliable embedded AI system.

01 🎙

Dataset collection

Personally recorded over 5,700 labelled audio samples across 30 different voices from varying age, gender, distance, and tone. Crucially, bathroom sounds like running taps and flushing were included as noise data so the model could ignore them.Background noise and pronunciation variations were considered to improve robustness during inference

02 ⚙️

Feature engineering

Used Edge Impulse's MFCC block to convert raw audio into compact spectral features — stripping out redundant data and compressing the signal into something small enough for a microcontroller to process in real time.

03 🧠

Model training

A 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) was trained over 200 epochs using a learning rate of 0.005 to classify five output categories, including three predefined distress phrases, background noise, and unknown speech. The final deployed model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 90.59%.

04 🔌

Deployment & casing

Exported as a C++ Arduino library, flashed to the board, and wired to three LEDs with appropriate resistors — all housed in a custom 3D printed enclosure running off a 9V battery.

05 — Reflection

What I learned & what's next.

The biggest lesson was that data collection method matters more than model architecture. My first attempt used phone-recorded audio, while it was convenient the microphone characteristics didn't match the Arduino's built-in mic, causing frequent false positives when deployed. Switching to the board's own microphone for all recording made a significant difference. Multiple rounds of testing were conducted under different environmental conditions to evaluate detection reliability and system responsiveness

Including bathroom noise samples — tap water, toilet flushing, water droplets and any other background noise turned out to be one of the most important decisions. Without them, the model couldn't reliably filter out the environment it's actually deployed in.

I also learned that collecting data from 30 different voices, spanning different ages, genders, and speaking distances, was tedious but essential. A model trained only on your own voice will fail in the real world.
